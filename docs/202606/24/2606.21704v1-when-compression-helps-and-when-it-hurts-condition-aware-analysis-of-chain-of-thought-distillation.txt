Abstract 

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) distillation transfers multi-step reasoning from large reasoning mod-els to smaller students, but verbose teacher traces inflate both training and inference cost. Existing CoT compression methods fall into two families, selective pruning and generative rewriting, yet prior studies have left key factors entangled: granularity is confounded with im-portance criteria in pruning, restructuring level is rarely isolated in rewriting, and compression budgets are not systematically evaluated across domains or regimes. We recast CoT compres-sion along three dimensions: importance crite-rion, restructuring level, and compression bud-get. Sweeping these across two model families, Math and General domains, and Long-/Short-CoT regimes, we find that (i) importance cri-terion utility is strictly governed by granular-ity: step-level criteria converge on a shared rea-soning backbone, while token-level pruning re-quires symbol-aware signals to preserve the log-ical core; (ii) restructuring level inverts across domains: Math degrades monotonically with structural disruption, while aggressive rewrit-ing acts as a denoiser on General tasks; (iii) training-time compression does not necessarily translate to inference-time savings: Long-CoT students retain verbose habits despite concise supervision, making the training ratio an opti-mistic lower bound on deployment cost. These findings yield condition-aware guidelines for matching compression to deployment context. 

1 Introduction 

Large reasoning models (LRMs, Chen et al. 2025a) have recently achieved strong performance on com-plex reasoning tasks (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2024). Because directly deploying these models is costly, a common strategy is to distill their reasoning traces into smaller student mod-els (Huang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). How-

ever, these traces are often verbose, containing re-dundant verification, speculative detours, and re-peated reformulations (Chen et al., 2025d; Wang et al., 2025c; Hong et al., 2026). Such verbosity in-creases training-time token consumption and may also teach students to generate unnecessarily long reasoning traces at inference time. CoT compres-sion therefore aims to shorten reasoning supervi-sion while preserving the information needed for effective distillation. Existing CoT compression methods generally fall into two families. Selective pruning deletes parts of the original trace using saliency, uncer-tainty, surprisal, or related importance scores (Pan et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b). Generative rewriting produces a new shorter rationale through paraphrasing, step fusion, or structured regeneration (Yu et al., 2025; 1

Kang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025b). While both families successfully reduce cot length, current research still lacks a unified understanding of what underlying factors actually drive effective compression . In particular, three critical issues re-main unresolved. First, for selective pruning methods, the role of granularity is insufficiently understood. Exist-ing approaches typically operate either at the step level (Li et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b) or at the token level (Xia et al., 2025), but granularity is of-ten entangled with the importance criterion itself. As a result, it remains unclear whether performance differences arise from the scoring signal or from the level at which compression is applied. Second, for generative rewriting methods, the role of restructuring level is rarely studied in iso-lation. Existing rewriting approaches implicitly introduce different degrees of structural transfor-mation (Yu et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025), ranging from light paraphrasing to aggressive regeneration of reasoning traces. How-ever, prior work neither isolates restructuring level as an independent variable nor controls it against compression ratio, making it difficult to determine whether performance differences stem from infor-mation preservation or structural disruption. Third, across both pruning and rewriting meth-ods, the role of compression budget lacks system-atic evaluation. Existing studies typically report token reduction and downstream accuracy under a single task domain or compression setting (Han et al., 2024), without examining how compres-sion behavior changes across domains, reasoning regimes, or compression intensities. Collectively, these gaps reduce CoT compres-sion to trial-and-error tuning rather than princi-pled design. In this work, we address these lim-itations by reframing CoT compression as a con-trolled design problem along three dimensions (Fig-ure 1): importance criterion , which determines what reasoning content is retained; restructuring level , which characterizes how strongly the original reasoning structure is modified; and compression budget , which distinguishes the target compres-sion ratio, the realized training ratio, and the actual inference-time token ratio during deployment. We systematically study these dimensions across two model families, two task domains, and both Short-CoT and Long-CoT supervision regimes. Our analysis reveals the following principal find-ings. (i) Importance criterion utility is strictly gov-erned by granularity: at the step level, distinct crite-ria converge on a shared reasoning backbone, ren-dering criterion choice largely irrelevant; at the token level, this robustness collapses, and only symbol-aware signals preserve the logical core un-der aggressive pruning. (ii) The effect of restruc-turing level inverts across domains: on Math, accu-racy degrades monotonically as structural disrup-tion increases, leaving structure-preserving meth-ods as the only reliable choice; on General tasks, aggressive rewriting acts as a denoiser, matching or exceeding structure-preserving baselines. (iii) 

Training-time compression does not necessarily translate into inference-time efficiency. Under Short-CoT supervision, students learn genuinely concise reasoning patterns and achieve real deploy-ment savings. Under Long-CoT supervision, how-ever, students retain verbose reasoning behaviors even when trained on compressed traces, causing the realized inference cost to remain substantially higher than the nominal training compression ratio. These findings lead to a practical guideline for choosing CoT compression strategies. For sym-bolic or math-heavy tasks, compression should pre-serve the original reasoning structure, and token-level pruning should use symbol-aware criteria that protect constraints, operators, and intermediate val-ues. For general-domain reasoning, more aggres-sive semantic fusion or framework-level rewriting can be beneficial because it can remove noise with-out destroying a unique derivation path. When de-ployment efficiency is the goal, practitioners should measure the student’s actual inference length rather than relying only on compressed training-token counts. Overall, our study shows that effective CoT compression is not a universal token-reduction recipe, but a set of condition-aware choices that must be matched to the structure of the task and the intended deployment setting. 

2 Related Works 

Overthinking in LRMs Recent LRMs, like OpenAI’s o1 (OpenAI, 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), have demonstrated strong complex-task performance. Distilling their reasoning traces enables smaller models to acquire reasoning abilities (Huang et al., 2024). However, distillation also transfers the teacher’s overthinking behavior (Chen et al., 2025d)—redundant verifi-cation, speculative detours, and repetitive restates that inflate both training-time token budgets and 2inference-time latency without significant scaling benefits in student performance (Wang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025c). Motivated by this, recent work on efficient CoT explores ways to retain the utility of explicit reasoning while re-ducing its cost (Sui et al., 2025a). A complemen-tary line of work tackles overthinking from a model-architecture perspective, examining the effective-ness of pruning strategies on reasoning-augmented LRMs (Ding et al., 2026) and developing dynamic layer pruning that adaptively allocates computa-tion per token (Zhao et al., 2025a). In parallel, a growing body of work studies CoT compres-sion by pruning intermediate steps or compress-ing teacher traces via summarization and rewriting, thereby obtaining cheaper supervision for student training (Zhu and Li, 2025; Sui et al., 2025b). 

Trace-level CoT Compression: Selective Prun-ing and Generative Rewriting One thread, se-lective preservation and rationale pruning , retains critical trace segments: saliency-based estimators isolate informative spans to remove fluff (Pan et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025); information-theoretic sig-nals, uncertainty or surprisal, identify essential rea-soning blocks (Li et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025b); and dependency-aware frame-works preserve logically indispensable components by modeling inter-step relations through graphs or heuristic rules (Zhao et al., 2025b; Jia et al., 2025). A complementary thread, generative refine-ment and structural re-organization , paraphrases and condenses teacher traces across a transforma-tion spectrum—from skeleton-preserving polishing (Yu et al., 2025), to step-merging into abstract state-ments (Jiang et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2024), and intent-guided rewrites that replace the teacher tem-plate (Wu et al., 2025). Beyond discrete trace editing, recent work has also explored shifting rea-soning into continuous latent spaces (Chen et al., 2025c), with particular attention to designing effec-tive supervision signals for latent trajectories (Chen et al., 2026). Orthogonal to trace editing, length control constrains or predicts reasoning length, from lightweight inference-time constraints (Xu et al., 2025a; Ding et al., 2024) to budget-aware methods that distill budget-aligned traces as super-vision to internalize efficiency (Han et al., 2024). Our work systematically characterizes this com-pression spectrum to map the trade-offs between structural fidelity and distillation performance.

4.1 Empirical compression tolerance Long-CoT training exhibits higher empirical tol-erance. As shown in Figure 2, Long-CoT train-ing yields a larger budget of removable tokens: student accuracy stays stable across a wider range of compressing ratios compared to Short-CoT. This is not because long traces contain a specific, easily-identifiable class of “redundant” steps, such as re-flective statements(Table 1) as suggested by prior work (Jia et al., 2025). In fact, in our controlled experiments (Appendix E), pruning methods that deliberately target reflective statements perform no better than those that ignore them. Instead, redun-dancy in Long-CoT is diffuse: the critical reasoning skeleton is repeated and rephrased throughout the trace. Therefore, as long as that global skeleton sur-vives, moderate compression is robust, irrespective of which specific elaborations are removed. 

Domain-Dependent Sensitivity. General bench-marks sustain accuracy under much stronger prun-ing than Math benchmarks. This is because Gen-eral tasks typically accept diverse valid rationales and do not hinge on a specific intermediate state. Consequently, performance slowly degrades even when the training signal is partially corrupted. Math tasks, by contrast, require internally consis-tent intermediate quantities and precise constraint satisfaction. Small errors introduced by compres-sion are amplified down the reasoning chain, lead-ing to earlier and sharper accuracy drops under aggressive pruning 2.

4.3 Granularity Modulates Importance Criterion Utility 

The utility of an importance criterion depends largely on the granularity of the deletion unit. 

Step-level pruning: Convergence on the back-bone. Despite their different scoring metrics, step-level strategies based on Entropy, NLL, and LLMLingua2 yield similar accuracy. We analyze the pairwise Jaccard overlap of their pruning de-cisions, as reported in Table 3. The key insight is that the criteria agree strongly on which steps to preserve, where the overlap is 70–80%, but dis-agree substantially on which steps to delete 5. This means that all criteria converge on the same critical reasoning backbone. The remaining steps are pe-ripheral and interchangeable; removing one versus another has little effect. Hence, step-level pruning is robust to the choice of importance criterion. 

Token-level pruning: importance score quality matters. In stark contrast, token-level pruning is highly sensitive to importance score quality. Un-der strong compression, LLMLingua2 maintains a smooth accuracy curve, while Entropy and NLL baselines collapse, especially on Math. This di-vergence comes down to whether the criterion pre-serves local structural integrity. As shown in Sec-tion 4.2, LLMLingua2 preferentially retains sym-bolic anchors and syntactic structures. Probability-based heuristics often indiscriminately delete key operators and numbers alongside stopwords. This risks fragmenting the internal logic of a reason-ing step rather than trimming verbose phrasing, a pattern we confirm via coherence analysis 6.

5 Effect of Restructuring Level 

CoT compression changes not only length but also the organization of the reasoning chain. We use the 

restructuring level to describe how much a com-pressed CoT preserves the teacher’s step segmen-tation, local ordering, and high-level solution plan. We define a qualitative spectrum — from minimal to maximal perturbation — to characterize how each rewriting method restructures the reasoning path, while keeping compression ratio compara-ble. Each level below represents an existing class of rewriting strategies, with representative work cited in line with prompts and comparative cases in Appendix A and Appendix I: • L1: Structural Preservation (SP): The rewriter is constrained to only remove redundant words to target surface verbosity, strictly preserving the verbatim logical sequence (Yu et al., 2025). • L2: Structural Editing (SE): The rewriter per-forms step-level operations like keeping, deleting or rephrasing to create a more efficient but struc-turally similar reasoning path (Jiang et al., 2025). • L3: Semantic Fusion (SF): The rewriter is en-couraged to retain only “key information” to fuse multi-step sequences into novel statements (Kang et al., 2024). • L4: Framework Replacement (FR): The rewriter discards the original structure to regen-erate reasoning via a predefined formal tem-plate (Wu et al., 2025). 

5.1 Robustness Across Restructuring Level 

As illustrated in Figure 3, a consistent pattern along this spectrum: across domains, lower restructuring levels are more robust as compression strengthens, whereas higher restructuring levels deteriorate ear-lier and show more domain-dependent variability. 

Robustness Degrades with Restructuring Level 

From SP to SE to SF as the restructuring level increases: at light to moderate compression, SP/SE/SF typically reduce rationale length while staying near the original baseline, particularly on General tasks. When compression becomes strong, however, SE and SF drop faster, while SP remains comparatively stable. A plausible explanation is that SP/SE primarily shorten surface form while retaining the step skeleton, whereas SF already be-gins to fuse across steps; further compression then more easily weakens information that cannot be re-constructed from the remaining text. At the highest restructuring level, framework replacement (FR) pushes this unstable effect further by rewriting the entire solution template, and we frequently observe higher variance across settings. 

5.2 Domain Sensitivity and Interaction Math Reasoning Demands Minimal Restructur-ing. In LLM-based summarization, we observe a domain divergence. On Math, SF and SE degrade significantly faster than SP under strong compres-sion, making SP the safest choice, as illustrated by representative failure cases in Appendix J. In Math 6domains, Accuracy declines monotonically as the restructuring level increases from SP to FR. This fragility stems from the strict logical dependencies in mathematical reasoning: errors introduced by disrupting step boundaries through Semantic Fu-sion (L3) or by editing and rephrasing steps Struc-tural Editing (L2) are amplified downstream. Con-versely, General tasks often tolerate a wider range of valid rationales. Methods at higher restructuring levels remain competitive because these tasks are less sensitive to how intermediate steps are pack-aged, allowing for more aggressive abstraction. 

FR: The High-Variance Extremity. FR ampli-fies this domain split. On Math, it is among the weakest strategies; on General, it frequently reaches parity with, or even surpasses, structure-preserving methods under tight budgets. As shown in Figure 3, the Framework Replacement approach collapses on logic-heavy Math problems but re-mains competitive in General domains. We at-tribute this to the rigidity of the solution space. Imposing a predefined framework (L4) on Math problems disrupts the precise logical flow of the original rationale, leading to derivation errors. In contrast, on General tasks, template-level rewriting can effectively reorganize and denoise the trace, yielding a more focused rationale. The effect of restructuring level inverts across domains: on Math, robustness degrades mono-tonically with restructuring level, leaving SP the only reliable choice. In General, this mono-tonicity breaks down: aggressive restructur-ing (SF/FR) can match or exceed structure-preserving baselines.

6.1 Stability and Performance Decay 

Our empirical analysis reveals that model accuracy remains remarkably resilient, staying within 90% of the original baseline across a significant com-pression interval. However, as shown in Figure 6, this stability is eventually followed by a sharp non-linear degradation. Once compression exceeds a critical threshold, performance collapses rapidly, suggesting a fundamental limit to how much in-formation can be discarded before the underlying reasoning logic is compromised. 

The compression resilience is modulated by two distinct factors. Rather than a uniform decay, we observe that mathematical reasoning tasks are inherently more fragile than general-domain bench-marks, yet this sensitivity is significantly counter-acted by long-CoT teachers, which systematically extend the viable compression range.

Summarization as intent-driven rewriting. Un-like extractive methods, summarization modulates length indirectly via its rewriting objective. We observe that realized lengths do not scale linearly with target ratios; instead, the teacher LLM, con-strained by linguistic fluency and structural coher-ence, converges to method-specific characteristic lengths. Thus, the target ratio signals compression intensity rather than a precise numerical constraint. 

Structural constraints define the length band. 

As shown in Figure 4, the choice of structural fi-delity dictates the achievable compression range (the band), while the target ratio only facilitates marginal adjustments within that band. In High-fidelity methods, such as SP, strict struc-tural constraints force the model to preserve the original reasoning skeleton, yielding a narrow, con-servative band. Looser prompts methods such as SF/SE, permissive constraints allowing cross-step fusion enable the model to fold multiple steps into single spans, shifting the band toward smaller ra-tios and widening its variance. This suggests that stronger structural constraints yield stable but con-servative results, while weaker constraints enable more aggressive but volatile compression. 

Free Ratio as a Natural Equilibrium. The free-ratio represents a method’s intrinsic set-point ab-sent of rigid constraints. Figure 4 confirms that this "natural" landing point often yields superior token efficiency, appearing as “star” points on the opti-mal frontier. In structure-preserving methods, this mechanism serves as a self-regularizer, ensuring the model settles at stable, moderate ratios. Al-though less protective for aggressive variants with naturally lower bands, the free ratio avoids the in-stability of forced compression. Thus, we propose using the free ratio as a primary configuration for ensuring consistent performance. 

6.3 The Gap Between Training and Inference 

We observe a positive correlation between the end-to-end inference token ratio and realized compres-sion ratio. As shown in Figure 5, this correlation is tight in the short-CoT regime but weakens un-der long-CoT regime. Generally, ρtarget serves as a viable proxy, but the efficiency translation varies significantly by regime. Short-CoT exhibits a near-linear correlation ( ρinference ≈ ρrealized ) along the diagonal, indicating successful internalization of concise reasoning styles. In contrast, the Long-CoT regime reveals a significant efficiency gap where 

ρinference > ρ realized . This divergence stems from a translation loss where verbose habits persist despite concise training supervision. Thus, for complex tasks, ρ serves as an optimistic lower bound rather than a precise predictor of deployment cost. 8As compression budget decreases, accuracy de-grades as a "plateau-then-drop", with resilience shaped by domain and source-CoT regime. More critically, efficiency translation is regime-conditional: Short-CoT compresses faithfully, while Long-CoT does not, as verbose habits per-sist despite concise supervision, making ρ an optimistic lower bound for deployment cost. 

Conclusion 

7 Conclusion 

We have reframed CoT compression into three de-sign dimensions: compression budget, importance criterion, and restructuring level. Each is governed by a distinct factor. Whether training-side com-pression translates to shorter inference depends on the source-CoT regime, with Long-CoT students retaining verbose habits despite concise supervi-sion. Criterion utility is governed by granularity: step-level methods converge on a shared backbone, while token-level pruning demands symbol-aware criteria such as LLMLingua2 to preserve the logi-cal core. Restructuring level acts in opposite direc-tions across domains, denoising General tasks but degrading Math monotonically. Together, these re-sults show that compression efficacy is conditioned on regime, granularity, and domain; no single strat-egy universally dominates. Effective deployment requires aligning each design choice with its spe-cific task context and inference constraints. 

Limitations 

Our experiments adopt LoRA-based fine-tuning as the training objective, as our goal is to under-stand how different compression strategies alter the supervision signal itself rather than to compen-sate for supervision degradation through stronger distillation objectives. Full-parameter fine-tuning and more aggressive objectives remain important directions for future work. Furthermore, our Math-/General partition is a pragmatic dichotomy that isolates two prototypical classes of structural sensi-tivity rather than an exhaustive taxonomy of reason-ing task types. Tasks such as code generation and deep-search reasoning may exhibit hybrid sensitiv-ity combining symbolic anchors with more flexible solution spaces, and we leave these as concrete directions for future work.

A.2 Component 1: Degree of Structural Transformation 

The {COMPRESS_STRATEGY_SECTION} in our base prompt is populated with instructions correspond-ing to the four levels of structural transformation defined in the main paper. 

Level 1: Structural Preservation (SP). The prompt instructs the model to only remove redun-dant tokens without altering the logical structure. 

The SP Compress Strategy Section 

- For this task, "pruning" means you should only remove redundant tokens. - The goal is to preserve the original structure and se-quence of every logical step. - This strategy explicitly forbids rewriting sentences, merging steps, or deleting any part of the reasoning path. - Conclude with the "ANSWER" section only for ques-tions with a definitive, boxable answer. Otherwise, omit it entirely. 

Level 2: Structural Editing (SE). The prompt grants the model step-level editing capabilities, in-cluding keeping, deleting, and rewriting/merging steps. 

The SE Compress Strategy Section 

You are an expert in logical reasoning compression. Your task is to refine and compress the provided THOUGHT PROCESS by treating it as a sequence of implicit logical steps and applying the following revision actions: 1. **Evaluate each logical component/step** within the THOUGHT PROCESS. 2. Based on your evaluation, decide how to act on it: * **KEEP:** If a step is necessary and concise, keep it. * **DELETE:** If a step is redundant, unnecessary, or logically flawed, remove it. * **SINGLE-STEP COMPRESS:** If a step is neces-sary but verbose, rewrite it more concisely. * **MULTI-STEP COMPRESS:** If several adjacent steps can be logically combined into a single, more effi-cient step, merge them. 3. Synthesize your revisions into a single, coherent, and fluent reasoning path that preserves the core logical progression to the final answer. Ensure the final answer is clearly stated at the end. 4. Conclude with the "ANSWER" section only for ques-tions with a definitive, boxable answer. Otherwise, omit it entirely. 

Level 3: Semantic Fusion (SF). The prompt encourages abstraction by instructing the model to retain only the essential "key info." 

The SF Compress Strategy Section 

- Retain the key info needed to solve the question and do not add additional info that is not included in the origin. - Conclude with the "ANSWER" section only for ques-tions with a definitive, boxable answer. Otherwise, omit it entirely. 

Level 4: Framework Replacement (FR). The prompt for FR marks a conceptual shift from "com-pression" to "reconstruction." This is explicitly signaled to the model by altering keywords in the base template itself. Specifically, the main 

TASK description is changed to "Re-evaluate and Reconstruct..." and the strategy section is reti-tled RECONSTRUCTION STRATEGY . This directs the model to abandon the original CoT’s structure and generate a new reasoning trace from first principles. 12 The FR Reconstruction Strategy 

1. **PRIMARY GOAL:** Your main goal is to **RE-PLACE** the original ‘THOUGHT PROCESS‘. You are **NOT** compressing or editing the original text. You are generating a superior, structured replacement from scratch. 2. **ASSESS COMPLEXITY:** First, analyze the provided ‘QUESTION‘ to assess its complexity (simple, moderately complex, or highly complex). 3. **CHOOSE FRAMEWORK:** Based on your assessment, choose **ONE** of the following three frameworks to generate a new thought process. 4. **FRAMEWORKS:** * **For simple problems:** **Analysis:** [Understand the core elements and goals of the problem] **Approach:** [Propose direct solution methods] **Summary:** [Concisely summarize the solution approach and key points] * **For moderately complex problems:** **Analysis:** [Understand the problem and identify key points and challenges] **Initial Approach:** [Propose preliminary solutions] **Reflection:** [Evaluate the pros and cons of the initial approach] **Improvement:** [Refine the solution based on reflec-tion] **Summary:** [Summarize the key points of the final solution] * **For highly complex problems:** **Analysis:** [Analyze various aspects of the problem and break down its structure] **Problem Decomposition:** [Break complex problem into manageable sub-problems] **Sub-problem Processing:** (Repeat for each sub-problem) - Sub-problem X: [Initial approach, Reflection, Improved solution] **Integration:** [Integrate sub-problem solutions] **Overall Reflection:** [Evaluate the integrated solu-tion] **Final Optimization:** [Make final optimizations] **Summary:** [Summarize key points] 5. **REFERENCE USAGE:** Use the original ‘THOUGHT PROCESS‘ (‘answer‘) only as a reference to understand the key values, steps, and final conclu-sion. **DO NOT** follow its structure, wording, or verbosity. Summarization methods leverage a powerful LLM Gemini2.5-Flash (Gemini Team, 2025) as a "rewriter" to generate a shortened trajectory ˜r, as shown in Eq. 1. To navigate this design space, we introduce the Degree of Restructing Level, a novel dimension that categorizes rewriting strate-gies based on how deeply they alter the original CoT’s logic. It spans four ordered levels: • L1: Structural Preservation (SP): The rewriter is constrained to only remove redundant tokens, preserving the original sequence of logical steps verbatim and targeting only surface-level ver-bosity. • L2: Structural Editing (SE): The rewriter performs step-level operations like merging or rephrasing to create a more efficient but struc-turally similar reasoning path. • L3: Semantic Fusion (SF): The rewriter is en-couraged to retain only "key information," allow-ing it to abstract a multi-step sequence into a single, novel statement, achieving a higher level of abstraction. • L4: Framework Replacement (FR): The rewriter discards the original structure entirely and regenerates the reasoning process following a predefined formal template, enforcing a com-plete structural overhaul. The specific prompts used to elicit these behaviors are detailed in Appendix A.

D Definitions, Pareto Construction, and Full Results 

This appendix details the formal metrics used to characterize the safe regime, the construction of the Pareto efficiency frontiers, and the complete numerical tabulations for all experiments. 

D.1 Effectiveness Criterion and Safe Regime 

Let Ateacher denote the accuracy of the uncom-pressed teacher model, and let A(r) denote the student accuracy achieved at a realized compres-sion ratio r. We define a compression level as 

effective if it retains at least 90% of the teacher’s performance: 

A(r) ≥ 0.9 · Ateacher . (13) The safe regime is defined as the maximal contigu-ous interval of realized ratios starting from r = 1 .0

(no compression) for which this condition holds. All token counts reported ( T ) include both the input prompt and the generated reasoning tokens, using the same tokenizer and decoding strategy across all comparisons. 

D.2 Pareto Frontier Construction 

To characterize the trade-off between deployment cost and performance, we analyze the Pareto fron-tier in the (Cost , Performance ) plane. Let C be the set of all experimental configurations (points). A point i ∈ C with inference token count Ti and ac-curacy Ai is considered Pareto-optimal if there exists no other point j ∈ C such that: 

Tj ≤ Ti and Aj ≥ Ai,

with at least one inequality being strict. Practically, this frontier is constructed by sorting all valid candi-dates by ascending token count and retaining only those points that establish a new strictly increasing maximum in accuracy. This frontier represents the upper bound of efficiency—the maximum accuracy achievable for a given inference budget.

F Detailed Analysis of Token Preservation 

To understand the qualitative behavior of different pruning signals, we categorize the tokens removed by each method. We implemented a Python-based classifier to map every deleted token into one of three mutually exclusive categories based on the heuristics defined below. 

F.1 Token Categorization Methodology 

We first tokenize text via whitespace and strip trail-ing punctuation. Each token is then classified se-quentially according to the following priority rules, strictly aligning with our evaluation script: 1. Class 1: Constraint-Bearing & Symbolic (High Priority). This class captures the struc-tural anchors of the reasoning chain. It includes: • Logic Markers: Key deductive connec-tives (e.g., "let", "assume", "implies", "therefore", "since", "if", "iff" ). • Math Operators & Symbols: All stan-dard arithmetic operators, comparators, and Unicode mathematical symbols (e.g., 

∈, ⊆, ∀, P). • LATEX Commands: Substrings indicative of formal notation (e.g., \frac , \boxed ,

\sqrt ). • Numerical Patterns: Any token contain-ing digits, including scientific notation and fractions. 2. Class 2: Stopwords. Tokens found in stan-dard English stopword lists (sourced from NLTK (Bird and Loper, 2004)/scikit-learn). 

Crucially , to prevent misclassification, any stop-word that also appears in the Logic Markers list (e.g., "if", "not") is removed from this set and forced into Class 1. Thus, Class 2 strictly repre-sents low-semantic syntactic glue. 3. Class 3: Content. All remaining tokens that do not fall into Class 1 or Class 2. These are typically domain-specific nouns, verbs, and ad-jectives describing the problem context.

F.3 Validation of the Symbolic Anchor Hypothesis 

To further verify that loss of symbolic structure in NLL-based degradation, we conducted a controlled experiment with a restricted pruning scope. In this setting, we excluded tokens categorized as math-like patterns from the pruning candidate set entirely. Consequently, the NLL-based ranking and deletion were applied solely to the remaining vocabulary (i.e., stopwords and other content tokens), while all math-related tokens were preserved by default. As shown in Table 13, this selective retention strategy significantly improved the stability of NLL-based pruning. By simply preventing the re-moval of these symbolic anchors, the optimized NLL baseline recovered a large portion of the per-formance gap relative to LLMLingua2. This re-sult confirms that the standard NLL metric fails in high-risk settings specifically because it assigns low probabilities to essential symbolic operators, not because the metric itself is incapable of ranking redundant prose.

H LLM-Based Coherence Judge 

While quantitative metrics measure task accuracy, they do not fully capture the linguistic quality of the compressed traces. Our analysis suggests that token-level pruning—unlike step-level selec-tion—can severely disrupt local continuity and readability. While other method (Wang et al., 2025b) measures a token-level LM loss as a proxy of inter-chunk coherence, we are interested in how coherent a compressed CoT is as a teaching script for smaller models, so we implement an LLM-based coherence judge to perform pairwise com-parisons. 

Task Definition. We evaluate the reasoning co-herence of two Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationales (Method A vs. Method B) generated for the same question. The judge is explicitly instructed to ig-nore the correctness of the final answer and focus solely on linguistic and logical flow. The output is a decision label in {A, B, TIE }.

Judge Implementation. We utilize a powerful LLM, gemini-2.5-flash (Gemini Team, 2025), as the judge. The prompt instructs the model to evaluate: (i) global structural organization, (ii) log-ical continuity between steps, (iii) proper usage of intermediate conclusions, and (iv) the absence of disjointed or "broken" text. 

The pairwise comparison prompt used for the coher-ence judge.

Your goal is to decide which CoT is more coherent and easier to follow **as a reasoning process**, ignoring whether the final answer is correct.

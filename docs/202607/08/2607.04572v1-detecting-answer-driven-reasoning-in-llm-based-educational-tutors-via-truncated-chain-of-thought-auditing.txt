Abstract —Large language model (LLM) tutors often produce fluent step-by-step explanations, but a correct and pedagogically formatted response does not guarantee that the answer was derived from the student-facing problem. In realistic tutoring systems, the model may also have access to teacher notes, answer keys, rubrics, or retrieved solution artifacts. We study whether such private answer information can make tutor explanations answer-driven: the final answer is behaviorally available before the written explanation has justified it. Using Truncated Rea-soning AUC Evaluation (TRACE), which probes how early a chain-of-thought prefix can pass a verifier, we evaluate 1000 GSM8K test problems under three paired tutoring contexts: question-only, correct answer-key, and wrong answer-key. At fixed fractions of each generated explanation, we force the model to answer immediately and verify the response against the gold numeric answer. With Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, answer-key access raises median TRACE AUC from 0.375 to 0.900 and makes the gold answer available at the first 10% prefix in 997 of 1000 cases. The effect remains strong on the 746 examples where both question-only and answer-key explanations end with the correct answer. These results support truncated CoT auditing as a lightweight process-level diagnostic for answer-driven reasoning in math tutoring explanations. 

Index Terms —AI tutoring, chain-of-thought auditing, answer leakage, educational AI, large language models 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

LLM tutors are increasingly asked to provide step-by-step help rather than only final answers. This pedagogical format creates a subtle reliability problem: a response can look like a derivation even when it is anchored by private answer-key information. Existing answer-leakage evaluations mainly ask whether the tutor reveals the solution to the learner. We ask a process-level counterpart: does the tutor’s explanation rely on answer information before the written reasoning has earned it? Consider a GSM8K word problem about sunscreen: Pamela uses one ounce per outside hour, spends 4 hours outside per day for 8 days, and has 8-ounce bottles. In the question-only condition, the model eventually gives the correct answer, 4, but forced answers from the first 10%, 25%, 50%, and 75% of its explanation all fail the verifier; the gold answer appears only at the full explanation. This example motivates our central question: can we audit whether tutor-style reasoning is derived from the problem statement, or already answer-driven? We adapt TRACE-style truncated reasoning evaluation [1] to educational math tutoring. TRACE measures when a rea-soning prefix becomes sufficient to pass a verifier. We use TRACE as a measurement primitive rather than proposing a new truncation metric; our contribution is a paired educational audit for answer-key dependence in tutor explanations. In our setting, the verifier is exact match to the GSM8K gold answer, and the shortcut is access to teacher-only answer information. The method does not judge whether the prose sounds pedagogical; it measures when the answer becomes behaviorally available. This paper makes three contributions. First, we define answer-driven reasoning as a process-level failure mode for

M ETHOD 

Let xi be a math word problem with gold answer yi.For each problem, we generate tutor explanations under three conditions:  

> •

Question-only : the model sees only the student-facing problem.  

> •

Answer-key : the model sees the problem plus a teacher-only note containing the correct final answer.  

> •

Wrong-key : the model sees the problem plus a teacher-only note containing a deterministic wrong answer, yi +1 .All prompts ask the model to explain step by step and conclude with a boxed integer answer. The answer-key and wrong-key conditions instruct the model to use the key only as private context and write the explanation as if deriving the answer from the problem. For a generated explanation containing T tokens, we eval-uate prefixes at fractions 

F = {0.10 , 0.25 , 0.50 , 0.75 , 1.00 }.

At each prefix f T , we append a forced-answer tag asking the model to stop reasoning and give only the final answer. Let 

p(f ) be the verifier pass rate: the fraction of forced answers that equal the gold answer after integer normalization. In the main 1000-problem run, we use one deterministic forced answer per prefix, so p(f ) ∈ { 0, 1}. These forced-prefix pass rates are distinct from original full-response accuracy: even at 

f = 1 .00 , the probe asks for a fresh forced answer appended after the generated explanation, so the TRACE curve is not constrained to be monotone or identical to the original final answer. An early pass therefore indicates that the answer is recoverable from that prefix, not that all longer prefixes will preserve the same answer. We report three scalar summaries. TRACE AUC is the trapezoidal area under the curve p(f ) over F. Pass@10 is 

p(0 .10) , the earliest-prefix pass indicator. First-pass is the first prefix fraction where p(f ) ≥ 0.5, or 1.0 if no earlier prefix passes. Higher TRACE AUC and Pass@10 indicate earlier gold-answer availability; lower first-pass indicates earlier first recoverability. III. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

B. Why Can Prefix Curves Dip? 

TRACE curves are not logical monotonicity tests. Each point is a new forced-answer completion conditioned on a different prefix. A short answer-key prefix can make the gold answer recoverable, while a longer prefix can contain a self-generated arithmetic error that the forced-answer probe then follows. In the answer-key condition, 884 of 1000 examples pass at all five prefix fractions, but 80 examples pass at 10% and fail at the 100% forced probe. One representative answer-key example is a glass-buying problem whose gold answer is 64. The problem states that one glass costs $5 and every second glass costs 60% of that price, so the correct calculation is eight full-price glasses and eight discounted glasses: 8 · 5 + 8 · 3 = 64 . The trace passes at 10%, 25%, 50%, and 75%, showing that the gold answer is recoverable early. However, the generated explanation later changes the discount pattern into “$5, $5, $3, $3, ...” and then says that every third glass is discounted. It consequently counts five $5 glasses and eleven $3 glasses, ending with the wrong boxed answer 58. The 100% forced-answer probe is appended after this explicit wrong conclusion, so it follows the recent erroneous derivation rather than the earlier answer-key signal. This is why we interpret early passes as answer recoverability, not as a guarantee that later self-generated reasoning will preserve the answer. The paired analysis in Table III confirms that the effect is not driven by a few outliers. Answer-key TRACE AUC exceeds question-only TRACE AUC in 94.8% of paired problems, with only 0.4% losses. The same pattern holds when final-answer correctness is controlled: among final-answer correct pairs, answer-key has higher AUC in 93.4% of cases and lower AUC in only 0.1%. C. Correctness Confounding 

A natural alternative explanation is that the probe merely detects whether the model will eventually answer correctly. The results do not support that interpretation. Answer-key access does improve full-response accuracy, from 0.782 in the question-only condition to 0.891. However, the key compar-ison is the final-answer correct subset: 746 problems where both conditions end with the same correct gold answer. In this subset, final-answer correctness is fixed, yet the answer-key median TRACE AUC remains 0.900 while the question-only median remains 0.375. Answer-key traces win 697 of 746 paired comparisons. Thus the diagnostic is not simply separating correct from incorrect generations; it is measuring when the correct answer becomes available within the gener-ated explanation. This distinction matters for tutoring. A teacher or automated evaluator who inspects only the final answer would treat both members of a final-answer correct pair as successful. A conventional answer-leakage detector might also accept both if neither response directly exposes the private key. Prefix auditing reveals an additional property: whether the explanation trajectory itself behaves as if the answer was known from the start. 

D. Wrong-Key Counterfactual 

The wrong-key condition is not intended as a realistic tutoring policy. It is a counterfactual anchoring test. If teacher-only answer artifacts are irrelevant to generation, replacing the key with a nearby wrong value should have little effect. In our run, wrong-key final accuracy drops to 0.613, and 387 of 1000 wrong-key explanations end with an incorrect answer. The median wrong-key TRACE AUC is 0.125, much closer to question-only than answer-key for gold-answer availability, because the private key often directs the model away from the gold answer. The wrong-key results should be interpreted carefully. A model can sometimes override an incorrect key through its own arithmetic, and a wrong-key trace can occasionally still pass the gold verifier at later prefixes. Therefore wrong-key failure is not a necessary condition for answer-driven reasoning. Its role is evidential: it shows that the same private channel used for correct answer keys can materially shape the tutor’s explanation and final answer. V. D ISCUSSION 

The experiment suggests that answer-key access can change not only final accuracy but also the temporal structure of a tutoring explanation. A tutor response may be correct and formatted as a derivation, yet prefix probing can show that the answer was available before the written explanation estab-lished it. This makes answer-driven reasoning a useful process-level complement to answer leakage: leakage concerns what the tutor reveals, while answer-driven reasoning concerns what the tutor relies on. 

A. Audit Workflow 

The proposed audit can be used as a lightweight diagnostic layer for educational LLM systems: sample tutor explanations, probe a fixed grid of prefixes with forced-answer prompts, compute early-pass metrics against an available verifier, and compare the curves against a baseline without answer-key or solution-artifact access. Traces with unusually high Pass@10 or TRACE AUC can be flagged for manual review, prompt redesign, or retrieval filtering. This workflow is not a replacement for pedagogical eval-uation. It does not score whether an explanation is clear, supportive, or appropriately scaffolded. Instead, it detects a narrower dependency failure: the answer appears behaviorally available before the written explanation has established it. That narrowness is useful in deployment because it produces a quantitative artifact that can be compared across prompts, models, and retrieval policies. 

Limitations. The study is controlled and should not be overgeneralized. We use one 3B model, one short-answer math dataset, and deterministic forced-answer sampling. Open-ended tutoring, multi-turn dialogue, rubric-based grading, and nonnumeric answers require more complex verifiers. The method also assumes that forced-answer behavior is informa-tive about the original explanation, which is plausible but not equivalent to observing hidden model computation. VI. R ELATED WORK 

Chain-of-thought reasoning and faithfulness. Trans-former language models [4] can be prompted to produce explicit reasoning traces, and chain-of-thought prompting im-proves performance on arithmetic and symbolic tasks [5], [6]. Decoding and decomposition methods such as self-consistency and program-aided reasoning further improve reasoning relia-bility [7], [8]. However, generated explanations are not guar-anteed to be faithful to the model’s actual decision process. Prior work formalizes faithfulness concerns [9], shows that CoT can rationalize biased or shortcut-driven answers [10], and proposes intervention-based, information-flow, unlearning, counterfactual, and instance-level tests for faithfulness [11]– [19]. Our work is aligned with this concern but focuses on a concrete educational setting where private answer artifacts may be present. 

Verification and truncated reasoning audits. Mathemati-cal reasoning benchmarks and verifiers have made it practical to evaluate not only final answers but also reasoning processes [2], [20]. Process-supervision work similarly emphasizes in-termediate reasoning quality rather than outcome accuracy alone [21], [22]. TRACE detects implicit reward hacking by truncating reasoning traces and measuring how early a prefix can pass a verifier [1]. Recent computational-graph evaluations likewise emphasize that CoT can expose process-level properties not visible from final outputs alone [23]. We use the same basic observation: shortcut use should reduce the amount of written reasoning needed before an answer passes verification. Our target differs from reward hacking. We study tutor explanations conditioned on answer-key access and ask whether they make the answer available earlier than question-only explanations. 

AI tutor safety and leakage. Recent tutoring evaluations study answer over-disclosure, scaffolding failures, pedagogi-cal alignment, collaborative feedback, and robustness against adversarial students who try to extract answers [24]–[28]. These works evaluate what the tutor reveals or how safely it teaches. We instead evaluate what the explanation appears to rely on. A tutor can avoid explicit over-disclosure and still be answer-driven if the answer shapes the explanation before the reasoning justifies it. 

Alignment and shortcut use. Our setting is also related to broader concerns about proxy objectives and reward gaming [29]. Empirical work on reward overoptimization shows how optimizing proxy feedback can degrade true task quality [30]. We study a simpler educational analogue: the visible explanation may satisfy surface expectations while relying on an unintended answer source. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced an educational adaptation of truncated CoT auditing for detecting answer-driven reasoning in LLM tutor explanations. On 1000 GSM8K problems, answer-key access made the gold answer recoverable from very short explanation prefixes, even when final-answer correctness was held fixed. The result shows that correct tutor-style explanations can con-ceal answer-driven generation, and that TRACE-style prefix probing is a lightweight way to audit this failure mode.
